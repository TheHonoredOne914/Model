Budgeting (Planning Expenses)
Budgeting means planning in advance how income will
be spent. A budget is a written plan that aims to balance
income and expenses. People often plan to save and make financial
decisions with a specific purpose in mind to be achieved in future.
For example, a family may decide how much money to save each
month or spend monthly on food, rent, school fees, transport, etc.
Other examples include:

 Saving for a school trip next month.
 Buying sports equipment after six months.
 Saving for higher education in the future.
 Setting aside money for emergencies.
Setting such financial goals helps people prioritise their needs and
make careful decisions regarding spending, saving, and investing.
One simple way to set goals is to ask:

 What do I want to achieve?
 How much money will I need?
 By when do I want to achieve it?
 What steps can help me reach this goal?
Budgeting helps control unnecessary spending, such as excessive
eating out or impulse shopping, and enables people to prioritise
important expenses. It also helps people live within their means by
balancing income and expenditure.

Investing (Growing Wealth)
Priya and Sameer both receive ₹500 as a birthday gift. Priya
keeps her ₹500 in a piggy bank at home. One year later, she
still has ₹500. Sameer puts his ₹500 into a savings scheme that earns 8%
interest a year. One year later, he has ₹540. Both are being responsible
with money, but are they doing the same thing?
Investing means putting your savings into assets with the aim of
protecting their value and generating returns. It is like planting a
mango tree—you do not get fruit immediately, but once you do, it
continues to provide fruit for many years. While keeping savings in
cash provides greater liquidity, investing helps increase wealth and
protects the value of money. It also allows money to grow faster than
the rate of inflation. For example, if a school bag costs ₹1,000 today, and
inflation is 6% per year, the same bag may cost about ₹1,060 next year.
If your money is simply kept at home and does not grow, you may not
be able to buy the bag as your purchasing power will fall over time.

a) Fixed Deposits (FDs)
Fixed deposits are among the most common and safest investment
options offered by banks and post offices. In a fixed deposit, a
person deposits a lump sum amount for a fixed period, such as one,
three, or five years. During this period, the bank or post office pays
a fixed rate of interest on this amount, which remains unchanged
throughout the investment period. Fixed deposits offer assured
returns and are suitable for people who prefer safety over high
returns.
Other government-backed investment options include the Public
Provident Fund (PPF) and small savings schemes, such as the National
Savings Certificate (NSC) and the Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana. These
schemes encourage long-term savings while providing safe and
reasonable returns.

b) Bonds
Bonds are investments through which people lend money to the
government or companies (issuers of bonds) for a fixed period.
Bondholders earn regular interest. For example, if you buy a bond
worth ₹10,000 that pays 5% interest per year, you will receive ₹500
as interest after one year. If the bond matures after one year, you
will receive ₹10,500 in total. In reality, the maturity period ranges
from 1 day to 30 years or more. You may also be able to sell the
bond before maturity if you need the money earlier, making them
more liquid. Government bonds are generally considered safer
than corporate bonds and are suitable for those seeking a stable,
predictable income.

d) Mutual funds
Mutual funds pool money from many investors and invest it in a
basket of shares, bonds, or other financial assets. These investments
are managed by professional fund managers who decide where to
invest the money. If the investments perform well, investors earn
returns in proportion to the amount they have invested. However, if
the investments perform poorly, investors may incur losses.
Mutual funds are popular because they reduce risk by spreading
investments across different types of assets, a strategy known as
portfolio diversification. They are suitable for people who may not
have the time, knowledge, or experience to manage investments on
their own.

Protection and Risk Management
Life is full of uncertainties, such as illness, accidents, loss of
income, or damage to property. These unexpected events
can lead to significant financial losses. Therefore, an important
aspect of personal finance is protection—safeguarding your income,
savings, and assets against such risks.

Insurance
One of the most effective ways to achieve financial protection
is through insurance. Insurance is a legal contract in which an
individual pays a regular amount, called a premium, to an insurance
company. In return, the company agrees to provide financial
compensation for specific losses or expenses covered under the
policy. For example, a family may pay ₹500 every month for health
insurance. If a family member requires medical treatment, the
insurance company covers a large part of the hospital expenses.
Without insurance, the family might have to use a substantial portion
of its savings for the expenses incurred towards the treatment.
Insurance works on the principle of risk sharing. Many people pay
premiums into a common pool. In case of any unexpected loss,

Types of Insurance
a)

Health Insurance

Health insurance helps cover medical expenses, such as hospitalisation,
medicines, diagnostic tests, and treatments. It reduces the financial burden
of illness or accidents and allows families to focus on recovery rather
than worrying about medical costs. In India, government schemes such
as Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana provide health
insurance coverage to eligible low-income households.
b)

Life Insurance

Life insurance provides financial support to a family if the policyholder,
who may be the breadwinner of the family, passes away. It helps dependents
meet daily expenses, continue children’s education, and repay loans. Life
insurance is an important tool for ensuring long-term financial security.
c)

General Insurance

General insurance protects against losses related to vehicles, homes,
businesses, and other property. For example:

 Motor insurance covers repair costs and damages caused by vehicle
accidents.

 Property insurance protects homes and businesses against losses
caused by fire, theft, flood, earthquake, and other disasters.

 Crop insurance protects farmers from financial losses as it covers
damages caused by natural disasters, pests, and market price changes.

 Cattle insurance protects farmers and dairy owners against financial
loss in case of death or disability of their animals.

Taxes
Taxes are compulsory payments made by individuals and businesses
to the government to fund public services, such as schools, hospitals,
transport, defence, and other essential facilities. There are two main
types of taxes:

 Direct taxes, which are paid directly on a person’s or
organisation’s income or profits, such as the income tax.

 Indirect taxes, which are added to the price of goods and
services, such as the Goods and Services Tax (GST).

Personal Income Tax

Personal income tax is a tax paid on the income a person earns in a
year (called a financial year, say 1 April 2001 to 31 March 2002). All
citizens of India with a personal income are required to pay income
tax, with the sole exception of individuals who earn below a certain
limit and are exempt from paying the tax. As income increases, the
tax payable to the government also increases. This system is known
as progressive taxation, which aims to reduce income inequality
by imposing higher tax rates on individuals with higher incomes.
